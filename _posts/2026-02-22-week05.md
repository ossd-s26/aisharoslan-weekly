## Presentations
This week was a really fun one. I got to learn about all the creative and useful Chrome and Firefox extensions that other groups have been building over the past few weeks. I enjoyed seeing the variety of projects, from ones inspired by frustrations people had with their browsers, to projects showing NYU pride, and others that integrated AI agents into their extensions. It was exciting to see how much effort everyone put into building something new, especially since most of us didn’t have prior experience developing browser extensions.

When it came to presentations, many groups approached them like product pitches, almost as if they were presenting to potential users or investors. That style can definitely be attention-grabbing, especially when introducing a new application and trying to highlight its value. While I’m not sure that kind of pitch was completely necessary in a classroom setting, it was still enjoyable to watch and made the presentations engaging. <!--more--> 

My biggest takeaway from working with my own group was realizing how important it is for every team member to truly understand the application and the motivation behind building it. When everyone can clearly explain the purpose of the project and how it works, it makes the presentation much more convincing and shows that the team genuinely collaborated on the project.

From watching other groups, my biggest takeaway was the value of trying things outside your comfort zone, even if they seem unfamiliar at first. Projects like MindMelt took an unconventional approach, TabDown addressed a specific frustration with a clever solution, and the AI-integrated extensions showed how useful and innovative these tools can be. Seeing these projects made me realize that experimenting with new ideas and technologies can lead to really interesting results.

The three videos I watched were great exposure to professional presentations at a summit/conference. Kelsey Hightower’s keynote felt similar to a TED Talk. He walked through an idea by using an everyday example—like tailoring his sports coat—to illustrate a larger concept. His approach felt very natural and conversational, yet still professional and engaging. It showed how powerful storytelling and relatable examples can be when explaining technical ideas.

In contrast, Craig McLuckie’s presentation felt closer to a traditional talk. He walked through his slides clearly and spoke with a lot of confidence. His delivery showed how effective structure and clarity can be when presenting complex information, especially in a conference setting where the audience expects a clear explanation of ideas.

The talk by Linus Torvalds was especially interesting because of how unique the format was. Instead of standing and presenting alone, he sat down with another person who asked questions and guided the discussion. This interview-style format seemed to make the presentation feel more comfortable and natural, while also helping the conversation flow smoothly. It showed that presentations don’t always have to follow the same format to be effective.

Observing these presentations helped me think about areas where both my team and I can grow when presenting. Seeing different styles made it clear that there isn’t just one “correct” way to present. Some presenters rely on storytelling, others on structure and confidence, and others on conversation and interaction. Each approach can work well depending on the speaker and the context.

Based on these observations, I think my own presentation style can improve by becoming less rigid and more conversational. I also want to try using everyday examples to make ideas easier to understand and more relatable. Another important improvement would be showing a deeper understanding of the topic through confident posture, clear explanations, and strong delivery.
